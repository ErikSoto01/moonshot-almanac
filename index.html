This week's sky

Tue, Sep 29 – Mon, Oct 5, 2026

Clock times for events are Central Time (CDT). “Look” times are your local clock time and work across the lower 48. Positions computed for the central U.S. (39°N, 95°W).

This guide covered Tue, Sep 29 – Mon, Oct 5, 2026. A new one goes up every Friday on our Facebook Page, and here soon after.

  1. Wed Sep 30Moon

    The Moon near the Pleiades

    Before dawn, around 6:15 a.m.

    Before dawn, around 6:15 a.m., face west-southwest and look about six fists above the horizon: the Moon and the Pleiades are about 5° apart, less than a fist. Binoculars show the cluster's tight knot of stars best. The pair shifts a little each night as the Moon moves.

  2. Sat Oct 3Moon

    Last Quarter Moon

    8:25 a.m. CDT

    Half lit, rising around 11:15 p.m. and high in the south at dawn. Evenings are Moon-free for the week ahead, which is good for stargazing.

  3. Sun Oct 4Planet

    Saturn at opposition

    Early morning, around 7 a.m. CDT

    Earth passes between the Sun and Saturn, so it's at its brightest of the year, rises at sunset and is up all night. Around 9:30 p.m., face east-southeast and look about three fists above the horizon for a steady golden point. Distance: about 784 million miles.

  4. Sun Oct 4Moon

    The Moon near Pollux

    Before dawn, around 6:15 a.m.

    Before dawn, around 6:15 a.m., face east-southeast and look about six fists above the horizon: the Moon and Pollux are about 4° apart, less than a fist. The pair shifts a little each night as the Moon moves.

  5. Mon Oct 5Moon

    The Moon near Mars

    Before dawn, around 6:15 a.m.

    Before dawn, around 6:15 a.m., face east-southeast and look about five fists above the horizon: the Moon and Mars are about 4° apart, less than a fist. Mars is a steady orange-red point. The pair shifts a little each night as the Moon moves.

About

How Moonshot Almanac works

Moonshot Almanac is an independent Facebook Page for people who'd like to know what's up there tonight, and the true stories behind it. It's made for U.S. skies and U.S. times.

Faceless by design
There's no host on camera. The graphics, star charts and animations are made for the Page, and the sky positions are computed, not guessed.
Sources on every post
Captions name where each fact comes from, usually NASA, the Smithsonian, or the observatory behind the result. Sky times are calculated with Astronomy Engine and checked against published almanacs.
AI narration, disclosed
Our videos are narrated by an AI voice, and every Reel carries Facebook's AI label. A person researches and checks each script before anything is posted.
Calm, no hype
We don't call things "rare" or "once in a lifetime" unless they are, and we're honest about moonlight and clouds.
Corrections
If we get something wrong, we fix the caption and pin a comment that says what changed. We never delete a mistake silently. Spotted one? Send the Page a message.
